A defence strategy is, in the end, a procurement strategy: every capability a state commits to is something it must buy. Ireland has never intentionally created a defence acquisition strategy, meaning that it is building a defence capability decision by decision, across more than €1 billion in commitments since 2024. This paper outlines the strategy Ireland is actually using, prices it, and sets out the architecture that would let the State make its own procurement decisions rather than have them happen to it.

> **Key Finding** — In the first week of August 2026, the State published its first-ever national procurement strategy and prepared to sign acquisition orders for the largest defence procurement programme in its history. Neither document acknowledges the other. Three case studies — the Military Radar Programme, the Falcon 6X government jet, and the Presidency counter-drone purchase — reveal a single underlying process: procurement decisions that happen *to* Ireland's system rather than being made *by* it. The recurring cost of this arrangement plausibly runs to several hundred million euro across the current pipeline, while the institutional solution costs under €30 million a year.

## The revealed strategy

On 5 August 2026, the Minister for Public Expenditure launched *Procuring for our Future*, Ireland's first National Public Procurement Strategy — five pillars, fifty-one actions, an implementation group, a mid-term review in 2028. One of the first questions he faced was about an aircraft: whether the State's new €53 million government jet could land in fog, because it had been bought without an Israeli-made vision system that the Government's own supplier policy excluded. The minister responsible for how the State buys things could only relay another department's assertion that there were "no limitations on the aircraft's operational capability" — because no institution exists in the Irish state that could have given him the analysis he needed.

That incident is demonstrative of this paper's finding across the board. Ireland now has a comprehensive, consulted, KPI-tracked strategy for procurement across the public system — and no strategy at all for the procurement that is the largest per contract, hardest to run, and least forgiving of error. The new National Strategy's glossary defines public procurement as purchase "through a competitive tendering process". By that definition, every major defence acquisition of the past two years falls outside its conceptual universe.

The consequences are hard to overstate, because procurement sounds administrative. In the defence domain it is not: defence procurement *is* the defence strategy. The €1.7 billion capital programme is not the implementation detail of Irish defence policy; it *is* Irish defence policy, expressed as purchase orders. And because the State is the only customer for defence goods, its contract decisions determine not only what the Defence Forces can do, but whether an Irish defence industrial base exists at all.

The pattern the acquisitions form is set out in *Figure 1*. As a whole, they disclose an unwritten strategy with two components: **execution is outsourced** — the radar and armoured vehicle programmes are run government-to-government, with procurement carried out by the Direction Générale de l'Armement (DGA), the French armaments directorate — and **policy is improvised**, with the supplier exclusion that shaped the Falcon and counter-drone purchases applied contract by contract, at official level, without a published framework. ## What a procurement architecture is for

A mature acquisition system performs six functions: **price discovery** (competition reveals what things should cost), **requirements discipline** (an institutional check on specification drift), **industrial leverage** (participation requirements that convert public spending into domestic capability), **lifecycle management** (pricing the thirty-year cost of ownership before contracts are signed), **institutional learning** (each programme trains the people for the next), and **values implementation** (the apparatus that converts a foreign policy position into an operable supplier rule).

The Irish defence acquisition function has the apparatus for none of these. This is not a criticism of the officials involved: Ireland's defence procurement has historically been *sustainment* purchasing — vehicles, uniforms, ammunition — which the general framework handles with competence. What the State has never done until now is first-generation capability acquisition. There is no template, no institutional memory, and no personnel expertise for buying a radar network, because the State has never bought one.

Government-to-government contracts are expressly excluded from the EU Defence and Security Procurement Directive. The route Ireland has chosen for essentially its entire capital programme is therefore the one that removes every layer of procedural discipline simultaneously. When Ireland awards a €500 million contract without competition, the only mandatory check is Circular 40/2002 — which requires the State to *record* the decision in an annual list to the Comptroller and Auditor General, but not to *defend* it. The threshold is €25,000: the radar programme passes through the same mechanism as a €26,000 printer contract.

## Three case studies

### 1. The radar: route selection by incapacity

The Military Radar Programme is the flagship of the €1.7 billion capital allocation, with acquisition orders being signed with France, first deliveries expected in 2027, and a fully operational network by end-2028. The procurement team's assessment was that a government-to-government arrangement was "the only feasible way for Ireland to develop the required radar capabilities". That is not a procurement decision; it is an admission that the State's own system cannot buy this capability. Three costs ensue: **the agent conflict** — Ireland has appointed as its buying agent an organisation whose mandate includes promoting French arms exports, so the competition a tender would have generated between Thales, Leonardo, Hensoldt, or Raytheon never happened; **the leverage** — industrial participation can only be demanded of a supplier competing to win, so the highest-value procurement in the history of the State spends every euro abroad; and **the learning** — outsourced execution means Ireland's first capability build generates almost no Irish institutional memory.

The channel is also concentrating: sonar to Thales, the jet to Dassault, the armoured vehicles to Paris, the radar to the DGA. A state that spent two decades leaving one strategic dependency unacknowledged — on the United Kingdom for air policing — is acquiring a second, on France, for procurement capacity, while its declared bilateral security track runs through London. No published document explains why.

### 2. The Falcon: a position without a policy

In August 2024 the Government announced that Ireland would not purchase military equipment containing Israeli-manufactured products. The new €53 million Falcon 6X was accordingly ordered without FalconEye, the Elbit-manufactured vision system that assists landing in fog. Yet four months later the same Department signed a €91.7 million helicopter contract reportedly carrying Elbit cockpit systems, and the maritime patrol fleet flies Israeli-manufactured radar today. The policy's actual operating form: exclusion where a direct Israeli contract would be required; tolerance of embedded content through third-party primes.

The position exists only as ministerial remarks. It was never translated into policy, so no procurement process was built around it — nothing that identifies, for each acquisition, what the exclusion forecloses, what alternatives exist at what price, and where no workaround exists, what capability the State is giving up. The result is the worst of every world: Ireland paid a capability price, did not achieve the exclusion, and absorbed a week of international ridicule for a hypocrisy it could have rebutted with a single, stated policy — had any institution actually created it. The State has denied itself the ability ever to learn the price of its own positions.

### 3. The counter-drone purchase: the interaction under pressure

Days after unidentified drones were flown during President Zelenskiy's visit, the Government announced €19 million for a counter-drone system ahead of the EU Presidency — contracted by February 2026, in the one capability domain where the excluded Israeli suppliers are the global benchmark. The result looks like a success: a non-Israeli system was found. But the conditions — best suppliers excluded, time desperately short, no prior experience, NATO's purchasing scheme closed to a non-member — permitted only one outcome: a rushed purchase, with no competition, from the same Franco-European channel as everything else, at a price no rival bid ever tested. Was the Israeli system cheaper, better, or faster? The State cannot say — it never asked.

## The bandwidth tax

The costs of a missing procurement architecture are real, large, and nowhere accounted for: a **sole-source premium** (competition typically saves 10–20% against sole-source pricing, and no major Irish award since 2024 has faced a competing bid); an **intermediation rent** (government-to-government execution is not free — the US analogue charges a flat 3.2% surcharge; Ireland is paying for a procurement institution, just France's); a **forfeited industrial return** (a participation policy on the €2.5 billion decade pipeline would plausibly generate €375–750 million in gross domestic workshare); and **programmatic lock-in**, the largest component — the €1.7 billion capital programme implies roughly €2.5–4 billion in lifetime sustainment, all on terms negotiable only while competitive pressure still exists.

There is one counterfactual to weigh: a competitive tender run by an inexperienced state might have added two to three years, or failed outright, and the cost of airspace blindness through the EU Presidency is not zero. The government-to-government route may have been the right call for the radar. This paper's argument is that no published accounting demonstrates it was — and that the conditions that made it "the only feasible way" are being reproduced rather than repaired.

## What small states build instead

None of this architecture is novel. **Denmark** runs defence acquisition through DALO, a bounded agency of the Ministry of Defence. **Norway** split materiel acquisition out of its armed forces in 2016 to create Forsvarsmateriell, precisely to professionalise the buying function — and its sovereign wealth fund's Council on Ethics shows what a values-based exclusion regime looks like when it publishes criteria and reasoned decisions: it is respected even by those who disagree with it. **Finland** treats lifecycle costing as standard discipline in defence business cases. Each of these similarly sized states procures via a named institution with a published strategy. An Irish acquisition office would be bounded — built for a €1.7 billion programme, not a superpower arsenal — at an estimated €15–25 million a year, grafted onto the National Strategy's own Pillar 4 professionalisation infrastructure.

## What happens if Ireland does not act

The armoured vehicle deal — 200–300 vehicles from the French Army's Scorpion programme at a reported €500–800 million, the biggest Army equipment purchase in the history of the State — is progressing the same way as the radar, but is not yet signed. It is the last major contract where this apparatus could still change the terms. Without it: the sustainment terms are delivered by default, and the State becomes a price-taker to a sole supplier for the operational life of both programmes; the Department's Corporate Procurement Plan, due by Q2 2028, will codify a commodity-purchasing function at the precise moment it is executing a capability programme; the next values-based position enters the system with no framework, repeating 2024; and sole-source procurement consolidates into precedent, with the radar cited as the authority for every future direct award.

> **The Bottom Line** — Ireland's procurement gap is not only an institutional problem, but a fiscal one. The State is paying a bandwidth tax — embedded in every contract, yet absent from every budget justification. The strategy published in August 2026 proves the State can build procurement institutions when it decides to. The defence programme proves it has not chosen to build them where the money is largest and the stakes are highest. Creating a €30 million defence procurement institution is not a cost; it is the cheapest money in the defence budget.

## Recommendations

### 1. Publish a Defence Acquisition Strategy

A formal companion to the National Public Procurement Strategy, reporting through the Public Procurement Advisory Council: a route policy defining when government-to-government, when competitive, when joint or EU procurement — with justification standards for each. Before the Q2 2028 mid-term review.

### 2. Establish a bounded Defence Acquisition Office

On the Danish and Norwegian model, grafted onto the National Strategy's Pillar 4 professionalisation infrastructure, at roughly €15–25 million in steady state — so the learning from the current programme accrues in Dublin, not Paris. Phased from Budget 2027.

### 3. Attach industrial participation requirements now

Industrial participation and maintenance workshare requirements on all defence arrangements above €50 million, beginning with the radar sustainment phase and the remaining Scorpion tranches — before the acquisition orders complete in 2026–27, the only stage at which leverage exists.

### 4. Publish a Supplier Exclusion Framework

Criteria, supply-chain depth, capability impact assessment, ministerial waiver, periodic review — on the Norwegian Council on Ethics model. The State can hold its values and know their price. Q2 2027.

### 5. Upgrade Circular 40/2002 from listing to justification

An annual Dáil statement on defence acquisitions above €10 million with route justification and a value-for-money benchmark, plus dedicated C&AG defence audit capacity — so the premium becomes visible, and therefore contestable. Budget 2027 cycle.

### 6. Apply for SAFE

Route eligible procurement through joint European frameworks: Ireland as participant, not only chair, with scale it cannot generate nationally. Before the next SAFE window closes.
